# Part VI — Thermal, Electrical, Dielectric and Magnetic Materials

Parts III to V asked how materials carry load. This part asks how they respond to everything else: heat, electric fields and magnetic fields. A turbine blade survives because a ceramic coating barely conducts heat; a power line works because aluminium conducts electrons well and porcelain does not; a transformer core wastes little energy because its steel magnetizes easily. These **physical and functional properties** are what make materials do things rather than merely hold things up, and they decide the performance of energy systems, electronics, sensors, vehicles, buildings and medical devices.

The organizing idea of the part is the same as in the rest of the book: each property is traced to a structure. Heat is carried by lattice vibrations (phonons) and electrons; charge by electrons, holes and ions; polarization by displaced charges and dipoles; magnetism by electron spins and their exchange coupling. Once these carriers and their scattering are understood, the processing levers become clear: alloying scatters electrons and phonons, doping adds carriers, grain boundaries and domain walls pin or impede, and texture and anisotropy direct flows.

Every circuit is built from two kinds of material doing opposite jobs. Conductors must carry current with as little loss as possible through wires, busbars, contacts, interconnects and electrodes. Insulators must stop current and hold voltage without losing energy, for years, while hot, wet and dirty. A third group, the functional dielectrics, turns the electric field into something else: strain, charge from heat, a change of refractive index, or a very large capacitance per unit volume. This part asks why a material falls into one group or another, what sets the numbers an engineer reads on a datasheet (resistivity, temperature coefficient, permittivity, loss tangent, breakdown strength, piezoelectric coefficient), and how processing and service conditions move those numbers. The quantum theory of electrons in solids is deliberately held back until Part VII. Here we use the simplest model that works and say clearly where it stops working.

Electrical engineering also runs on magnetic flux. Every transformer in the grid, every motor and generator, every inductor in a power converter, every current sensor and every hard-disk read head depends on a material that guides, stores or produces flux. Some of these materials must give up their magnetization effortlessly, millions of times a second, wasting as little energy as possible. Others must hold their magnetization for decades against heat and opposing fields. Both are made from the same few ingredients — iron, cobalt, nickel, a handful of rare earths and oxides — and both are governed by the same physics of atomic moments, exchange and domains. What separates a transformer core from a permanent magnet is microstructure: how easily a domain wall can move through the material. Superconductors close the part: they carry current without resistance, but only when nanometre-scale defects pin the magnetic flux inside them.

**By the end of this part you should understand:**

- why heat capacity, thermal expansion and thermal conductivity differ so widely between materials, and how temperature changes generate thermal stress and thermal shock;
- why resistivity is set by carrier density and by scattering, and why scattering, not carrier density, separates good metals from poor ones;
- how temperature, alloying, cold work, grain size and film thickness each add their own resistivity, and when those contributions can simply be added (Matthiessen's rule);
- why a contact between two metals has resistance even when both are excellent conductors, and why contact force, hardness and surface films control it;
- why good electrical conductors are also good thermal conductors (Wiedemann–Franz), and which materials break that link;
- how a dielectric polarizes (electronic, ionic, orientational and interfacial mechanisms) and why permittivity and loss depend on frequency and temperature;
- what dielectric strength means, why measured values depend on thickness, time and electrode geometry, and how gases, liquids and solids break down;
- how moisture, contamination, tracking and partial discharge degrade insulation, and how thermal and electrical aging set insulation life;
- why only certain crystal symmetries allow piezoelectricity, pyroelectricity and ferroelectricity, and how these effects are used in capacitors, actuators, sensors, resonators and modulators;
- the relations between $B$, $H$ and $M$, what susceptibility and the several kinds of permeability mean on a datasheet, and why a gap or the shape of a part can matter more than the material's own permeability;
- why diamagnetism and paramagnetism are weak, and how the exchange interaction produces the spontaneous order of ferromagnets, antiferromagnets and ferrimagnets, including the spinel ferrites;
- why ferromagnets form domains, what sets the width and energy of a domain wall, and how wall pinning by defects and grain boundaries decides hysteresis, remanence and coercivity;
- how saturation magnetization, anisotropy and coercivity fall with temperature, and what the Curie and Néel temperatures mean for derating;
- where core losses come from (hysteresis, classical eddy currents, excess loss), how they scale with frequency, induction, lamination thickness and resistivity, and how the Steinmetz equation is used and misused;
- the engineering families of soft materials — electrical steels, ferrites, amorphous and nanocrystalline alloys, nickel–iron and cobalt–iron alloys, powder cores — and of permanent magnets — NdFeB, SmCo, hard ferrite and AlNiCo;
- why the energy product, the temperature coefficients and the heavy-rare-earth (dysprosium) content of a magnet decide whether a motor survives its worst operating point;
- how magnetic shields work at low and high frequency, and how materials are selected for transformers, motors, inductors and magnetic sensors;
- why a superconductor is more than a perfect conductor (the Meissner effect), and how the penetration depth, the coherence length and their ratio $\kappa$ separate Type I from Type II behaviour;
- why practical superconductors (Nb–Ti, Nb$_3$Sn, MgB$_2$, REBCO, BSCCO) are made as fine-filament composite wires or textured coated-conductor tapes, and how brittleness, strain sensitivity and reaction heat treatments constrain magnet design;
- why one structural feature (a solute, a pore, a domain wall) can change several properties at once.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part VI builds directly on Part I: the reciprocal lattice and Brillouin zone (Chapter 4) are the language of phonons and electron bands, tensor properties (Chapter 4) describe anisotropic conductivity, permittivity and piezoelectricity, and defects (Chapter 5) are the scatterers and dopants that set transport. Thermodynamics and diffusion (Part II) reappear in solid electrolytes, defect chemistry and thermal transients, which obey the same mathematics as Fick's laws. Thermal stress links back to the mechanical behaviour of Part III and the composite laminates of Chapter 23.

Chapter 25 treats conduction with a classical free-electron picture: carriers, a mean free time and a mobility. Chapter 31 replaces that picture with energy bands and shows why the carrier density of a metal is fixed while that of a semiconductor is not. Chapter 32 then reuses mobility and scattering for semiconductors. Thin-film conductors and contacts return in Chapter 34 (semiconductor contacts and films), Chapter 50 (metallization) and Chapter 51 (bonding wires, solders, conductive adhesives). The dielectrics of Chapter 26 reappear as gate dielectrics in Chapter 34, as PCB laminates and encapsulants in Chapter 51, and as failure mechanisms in Chapter 44. The ferroelectric and piezoelectric materials of Chapter 27 are needed again for actuators (Chapter 37), for energy harvesting (Chapter 40), for nanoscale and thin-film functional layers (Chapter 38), and for the material-selection methods of Chapter 58. The magnetic materials of Chapters 28 and 29 follow the same pattern as the dielectrics: domains, hysteresis, a Curie temperature and frequency-dependent loss. Chapter 28 traces each property to its structural origin: atomic moments and exchange set the saturation and the Curie temperature, crystal anisotropy and magnetostriction set the domain structure, and defects and grain size set coercivity. Chapter 29 follows those properties through processing — rolling and texture, rapid solidification, controlled crystallization, sintering, grain-boundary diffusion — to device performance and cost. Superconductors (Chapter 30), whose perfect diamagnetism and flux pinning are close relatives of the diamagnetism and domain-wall pinning of Chapter 28, close the part. The design of transformers, rotating machines and inductors themselves belongs to the Electrical Energy & Machines core, and the operation of sensors and spintronic devices to Electronics & Computing; this part supplies the materials those subjects take as given.

Forward, these properties are the basis of the semiconductor and photonic materials of Part VII, of the smart materials, nanomaterials and energy materials of Part VIII (shape-memory and piezoelectric actuators, quantum dots, batteries, thermoelectrics) and of high-temperature service in Chapter 41. Thin-film processing (Chapter 49), characterization (Part XI) and electronic-structure methods (Chapter 55) assume the vocabulary established here.
