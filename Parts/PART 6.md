# Part VI — Physical and Functional Properties

Parts III to V asked how materials carry load. This part asks how they respond to everything else: heat, electric fields, magnetic fields and light. A turbine blade survives because a ceramic coating barely conducts heat; a power line works because aluminium conducts electrons well and porcelain does not; a transformer core wastes little energy because its steel magnetizes easily; a solar cell, an LED and an optical fibre exist because a few materials absorb, emit or guide light exactly as wanted. These **physical and functional properties** are what make materials do things rather than merely hold things up, and they decide the performance of energy systems, electronics, sensors, vehicles, buildings and medical devices.

The organizing idea of the part is the same as in the rest of the book: each property is traced to a structure. Heat is carried by lattice vibrations (phonons) and electrons; charge by electrons, holes and ions; polarization by displaced charges and dipoles; magnetism by electron spins and their exchange coupling; optical response by how electrons and ions follow an oscillating field. Once these carriers and their scattering are understood, the processing levers become clear: alloying scatters electrons and phonons, doping adds carriers, grain boundaries and domain walls pin or impede, and texture and anisotropy direct flows. The treatment here is the whole-discipline view; the separate *Materials Core (EEE)* book takes the electronic, dielectric, semiconductor, magnetic and optical topics to the depth needed by electrical engineers.

**By the end of this part you should understand:**

- why heat capacity, thermal expansion and thermal conductivity differ so widely between materials, and how temperature changes generate thermal stress and thermal shock;
- how metals, ionic conductors, dielectrics and superconductors conduct or block charge, and what limits each;
- why semiconductors have the carrier concentrations and mobilities they do, and how growth and doping control them;
- how exchange interactions, domains and hysteresis produce soft and hard magnets, and where magnetic losses come from;
- how refraction, absorption, scattering and emission arise in materials, and how they are engineered in fibres, LEDs, lasers and photonic structures;
- why one structural feature (a solute, a pore, a domain wall) can change several properties at once.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part VI builds directly on Part I: the reciprocal lattice and Brillouin zone (Chapter 4) are the language of phonons and electron bands, tensor properties (Chapter 4) describe anisotropic conductivity, permittivity and piezoelectricity, and defects (Chapter 5) are the scatterers and dopants that set transport. Thermodynamics and diffusion (Part II) reappear in solid electrolytes, defect chemistry and thermal transients, which obey the same mathematics as Fick's laws. Thermal stress links back to the mechanical behaviour of Part III and the composite laminates of Chapter 23. Forward, these properties are the basis of the smart materials, nanomaterials and energy materials of Part VII (shape-memory and piezoelectric actuators, quantum dots, batteries, thermoelectrics, photovoltaics) and of high-temperature service in Chapter 33. Thin-film processing (Chapter 41), characterization (Part X) and electronic-structure methods (Chapter 45) assume the vocabulary established here.
