# Part IV — Metals, Ceramics and Construction Materials

Parts I to III built general principles: how atoms arrange themselves, how thermodynamics and kinetics select a microstructure, and how that microstructure resists elastic deformation, plastic flow, creep and fracture. Part IV applies those principles to the material families that carry most of the world's engineering load. A car body, a jet engine, a hip implant, a glass façade, a furnace lining and a concrete bridge are each made from a metal, a ceramic, a glass or a cementitious composite chosen because its structure can be controlled cheaply and reliably at the scale required. The question running through every chapter is the same: given a family of materials, which compositions and processing routes produce which microstructures, and which engineering jobs do those microstructures do best?

The four chapters move from the most versatile family to the most massive: steels, stretched by allotropy, martensite and alloying; non-ferrous alloys, which win on lightness, conductivity, corrosion resistance or temperature capability; ceramics and glasses, where flaw control and viscous forming replace dislocation plasticity; and cement, concrete, timber, asphalt and masonry, made in billions of tonnes, where cost, durability and carbon footprint dominate design.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- How the Fe–C diagram, heat treatment and alloying produce the microstructures of carbon, alloy, stainless and advanced high-strength steels and of cast irons, and how hardenability and surface hardening are designed.
- How precipitation hardening, solid solution, phase balance and single-crystal processing are used in aluminium, copper, titanium, nickel, magnesium and refractory alloys, and in intermetallics and high-entropy alloys.
- Why ceramics are brittle, how powders are formed and sintered into dense parts, how glasses form and are strengthened, and how glass-ceramics and refractories are engineered.
- How Portland cement hydrates, why the water–cement ratio controls concrete strength and durability, and how steel, timber, asphalt and masonry behave as construction materials.
- How to compare these families and choose among them for a given component, load, temperature and environment.

Chapter 15 comes first because steel is the reference against which other structural materials are judged and uses nearly every mechanism of Parts II and III. Chapter 16 carries precipitation hardening and high-temperature design into the light and exotic metals. Chapter 17 changes the governing physics from dislocations to flaws and viscous flow. Chapter 18 closes with materials whose structure is set by hydration chemistry, biology or bitumen rheology, and whose tonnage makes their sustainability a global question.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part IV draws directly on the phase diagrams of Chapter 7, the diffusion and transformation kinetics of Chapters 8–10, and the strengthening, creep and fracture mechanics of Chapters 13 and 14. It hands forward to almost every later part: polymers and composites (Part V) are compared with metals and ceramics and use them as fibres and matrices; functional ceramics and electrical steels reappear in Part VI; superalloys, refractory metals and ceramics return in extreme environments (Chapter 33); corrosion of steels, aluminium and reinforcement and the deterioration of concrete are the subject of Part VIII; casting, forming, powder processing, welding and additive manufacturing of these materials fill Part IX; and the property data gathered here are the raw material for the selection methods and lifecycle assessment of Chapters 48 and 49.
