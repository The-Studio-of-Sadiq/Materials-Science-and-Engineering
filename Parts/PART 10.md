# Part X — Processing and Manufacturing

Up to this point the book has treated microstructure mostly as something a material *has*: a grain size, a precipitate dispersion, a texture, a population of pores. Part X treats it as something a factory *makes*. Every engineering component starts as a liquid, a powder, a vapour or a slab, and on its way to a finished shape it is melted, deformed, sintered, joined, layered or deposited. Each of those steps imposes a thermal history and a strain history, and the microstructure, defects and residual stresses of the finished part are the record of that history. The same Ti–6Al–4V can be a forged disc, a cast housing or a laser-melted implant, with identical composition and different properties. This part explains why, by applying the thermodynamics and kinetics of Part II and the mechanics of Part III to the processes that industry actually runs.

The emphasis is on the *materials* side of manufacturing: what each process does to structure, which defects it produces, and which process windows avoid them.

**By the end of this part you should understand:**

- how casting, deformation processing and powder metallurgy each create a characteristic microstructure, and the defects (shrinkage, gas porosity, hot tears, laps, forming splits, residual porosity) that go with each route;
- why the same metal is hot worked, warm worked or cold worked depending on its homologous temperature, and how forming limits, anisotropy and texture decide whether a sheet can be pressed into a shape;
- how thermomechanical processing combines deformation and transformation to produce grain sizes and textures that heat treatment alone cannot;
- what a fusion weld does to the metal beside it, why carbon equivalent and diffusible hydrogen control cold cracking, and when solid-state welding, brazing, adhesive bonding or mechanical fastening is the better choice;
- how additive manufacturing builds parts from melt pools a fraction of a millimetre wide, why it produces fine, textured and sometimes porous microstructures, and how post-processing and qualification turn a printed part into a certified one;
- how thin films and coatings are deposited, how growth conditions set their density, stress and adhesion, and how semiconductor crystals and devices are made.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part X is where the earlier parts are cashed in. Solidification, diffusion, recrystallization and precipitation (Chapters 8–10) are the kinetic rules every process obeys; the phase diagrams of Chapter 7 and the steel and alloy metallurgy of Chapters 15–16 say which transformations a thermal cycle can trigger; dislocation theory and strengthening (Chapters 12–13) explain why deformation hardens and annealing softens; fracture and fatigue (Chapter 14) explain why process defects, not average properties, usually decide a part's life. Ceramic and polymer processing are treated with their materials in Chapters 17 and 21, and composite manufacturing in Chapter 23; this part points to them rather than repeating them. Looking forward, the defects and residual stresses introduced here become the starting points of corrosion and failure analysis (Chapters 43–44), the structures produced here are what the characterization methods of Chapters 52–54 are used to verify, process modelling reappears in the ICME framework of Chapter 56, and process selection and cost enter materials selection in Chapter 58 and lifecycle accounting in Chapter 59.
