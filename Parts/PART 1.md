# Part I — Structure of Materials

Every engineering property starts with the arrangement of atoms. A steel plate that stays tough in an Arctic winter, a single-crystal turbine blade that survives gas hotter than its own melting point, a zirconia hip head that resists cracking, a polyethylene pipe that holds pressure for fifty years and a piezoelectric ceramic that turns a voltage into ultrasound all owe their behaviour to structure: which atoms are present, how they are bonded, how they are arranged in crystals, glasses or chains, which imperfections that arrangement contains, and how grains and phases fill the component. Part I builds the language for describing that structure and the tools for reasoning about it. It introduces the central idea of the discipline, that **processing sets structure, structure sets properties, and properties set performance**, and then works down from the whole field to the atom and back up to the microstructure.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- what distinguishes materials science (explaining properties from structure) from materials engineering (designing structure through processing), and how the structure–processing–property–performance tetrahedron organizes both;
- how structure is distributed over length scales from bonds to components and over time scales from atomic vibrations to design lives, and why thermal activation links them;
- the property packages of metals, ceramics, glasses, polymers, composites, semiconductors and biomaterials, and how bonding explains them;
- what isotropy, anisotropy and homogeneity mean, and how property charts and performance indices turn a design requirement into a ranking of materials, with cost, criticality and embodied carbon as additional objectives;
- the main crystal structures of metals, ceramics, semiconductors and silicates, Pauling's rules, polymorphism, and how to name directions and planes with Miller and Miller–Bravais indices;
- how polycrystals, texture and grain size, glasses, semicrystalline polymers, liquid crystals and quasicrystals depart from the perfect single crystal;
- the symmetry operations, point groups, Bravais lattices and space groups of crystals, how to read the International Tables, and how stereographic projections and pole figures display orientation;
- the reciprocal lattice and diffraction, structure factors and peak broadening, and how symmetry constrains tensor properties such as conductivity, piezoelectricity and elasticity;
- the families of defects, from vacancies and dislocations to grain boundaries and precipitates, how they are quantified, and why they control the structure-sensitive properties.

Part I is the foundation for everything that follows. Part II (thermodynamics and kinetics) explains *why* a given structure forms and how fast it changes. Part III builds on slip planes, grain size, dislocations and ordering to explain strength, creep and fracture. Parts IV and V apply the same language to metals, ceramics, cement, polymers and composites; Part VI uses symmetry and tensors for thermal, electrical, magnetic and optical properties; Parts VII and VIII extend it to advanced materials and degradation. Part IX shows how processing sets these structures, and Part X returns to them through characterization, computation and selection.
