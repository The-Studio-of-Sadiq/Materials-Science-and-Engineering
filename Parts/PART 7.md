# Part VII — Semiconductor and Photonic Materials

Part VI treated electrons in materials as either free to move (metals, Chapter 25) or bound in place (dielectrics, Chapters 26–27). Semiconductors sit between the two. Their conductivity can be changed by a factor of $10^{10}$ through a few parts per million of impurity, switched by an applied field, and generated or absorbed as light, and that is why nearly every active electronic function is built from them. This part starts with the electronic structure of crystals, counts and moves the carriers, surveys the material families, and continues with the interfaces, thin films and optoelectronic materials from which real devices are made. It ends with optical and photonic materials, where the same ideas explain how glasses, crystals and polymers refract, absorb, scatter, emit and guide light. The aim throughout is the material, not the device: what sets the band gap, the mobility, the lifetime, the breakdown field and the thermal conductivity, how processing and defects change them, and how an engineer compares candidate materials before any circuit is drawn.

## By the end of this part you should understand

- Why electrons in a crystal occupy energy bands separated by gaps, and how the size and type (direct or indirect) of the gap follow from the atoms, the bonding and the crystal structure.
- How the density of states, Fermi–Dirac statistics and the effective mass set the number of electrons and holes, and why that number depends exponentially on band gap and temperature.
- How donors and acceptors control carrier concentration, how doping profiles are described, and where doping stops working (freeze-out, intrinsic conduction, degeneracy, compensation).
- What limits carrier mobility and velocity, how empirical models such as Caughey–Thomas describe them, and how drift, diffusion and the Einstein relation fit together.
- How carriers recombine (Shockley–Read–Hall, radiative, Auger), what sets the minority-carrier lifetime, and why a few parts per billion of a deep-level impurity can ruin a wafer.
- How silicon, germanium, the III–V compounds, silicon carbide, gallium nitride, the ultra-wide-gap materials, amorphous, organic and two-dimensional semiconductors compare, and how figures of merit rank them for power, RF, sensing and optoelectronics.
- How heterostructures, band alignment, surface and interface states, thin-film defects and gate dielectrics determine the behaviour of real device structures.
- How light is absorbed and emitted in materials, and which materials are used for LEDs, lasers, photodetectors, solar absorbers, high-frequency electronics and sensors.
- How refraction, absorption, scattering and emission arise in materials, and how they are engineered in fibres, LEDs, lasers and photonic structures.

## How this part connects to the rest of the book

Part VII supplies the electronic-structure language that the rest of the book uses. Chapter 36 carries the absorption and emission of Chapter 35 into refraction, scattering and light guiding in every class of material. Chapter 38 builds quantum confinement, graphene and the other two-dimensional materials on the density of states and effective mass of Chapter 31. Chapter 40 rests on absorption, recombination and lifetime from Chapters 32 and 35 for photovoltaic absorbers and on carrier transport for thermoelectrics. In Part X, Chapter 50 shows how crystal growth, oxidation, diffusion, implantation and deposition produce the doping profiles, defects and interfaces described here, and Chapter 51 takes up the thermal and mechanical loads that wide-gap power devices put on their packages. Chapter 44 follows how semiconductor defects and dielectrics degrade in service; in Part XI, Chapter 52 measures the quantities defined here (four-point probe, Hall effect, spectroscopy), and Chapter 58 turns the figures of merit of Chapter 33 into a general method of materials selection. Device operation and circuit design remain in the Electronics & Computing core, and atomic structure and chemical bonding in the Advanced Chemistry core. Part VII is the material layer beneath both.
