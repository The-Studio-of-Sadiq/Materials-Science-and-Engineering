# Part II — Thermodynamics and Kinetics

Part I described what materials are made of: crystal structures, their symmetry, the diffraction that reveals them and the defects that every real crystal contains. It did not explain why a particular structure forms, why a steel quenched from 900 °C is hard while the same steel cooled slowly is soft, or why an aluminium alloy grows stronger for a week after heat treatment and then stops. Those questions have two halves. **Thermodynamics** says which state a material is trying to reach: which phases, of which compositions, in which proportions, are stable at a given temperature, pressure and chemical environment. **Kinetics** says how fast it gets there, by which path, and what it leaves behind if it is stopped on the way. Almost every microstructure an engineer uses — tempered martensite, age-hardened aluminium, a directionally solidified turbine blade, a sintered ceramic, a glass — is a kinetic compromise frozen somewhere between the starting state and equilibrium. Part II supplies the tools to predict and control that compromise.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- how Gibbs energy and chemical potential govern equilibrium in solids and solutions, and how free-energy–composition curves decide which phases coexist;
- how miscibility gaps, ordering, interfaces and point defects enter the same thermodynamic framework, and how Ellingham diagrams predict oxidation and reduction;
- how to read and use unary, binary, ternary and computed (CALPHAD) phase diagrams, including the Fe–C and key ceramic systems;
- how atoms move through solids by diffusion, how fast, along which paths, and how diffusion is exploited in carburizing, doping and homogenization;
- how new phases nucleate and grow, how solidification sets casting microstructures and segregation, and how rapid cooling produces metallic glasses;
- how precipitation, spinodal decomposition, pearlite, bainite and martensite form in the solid state, how recrystallization and grain growth reset microstructure, and how TTT and CCT diagrams turn all of this into heat-treatment practice.

The chapters build on one another in a deliberate order. Chapter 6 provides the free-energy curves from which Chapter 7 constructs phase diagrams. Phase diagrams say where a material is going; Chapter 8 says how quickly atoms can carry it there. Chapters 9 and 10 combine both — a thermodynamic driving force and a kinetic rate — into the theory of transformations, first from the liquid and then within the solid.

This part is the hinge of the book. It turns the structural vocabulary of Part I into microstructures that can be designed, and it underlies everything that follows. The strength of metals in Part III depends on the precipitates, grain sizes and martensite produced by the transformations of Chapters 9 and 10. The steels, aluminium, titanium and nickel alloys, ceramics and cements of Part IV are defined by their phase diagrams and heat treatments. Polymer blends and crystallization in Part V reuse the same free-energy and nucleation arguments. Corrosion and oxidation in Part VIII start from Ellingham and Pourbaix diagrams and from diffusion through oxide scales. Casting, welding, additive manufacturing and thin-film growth in Part IX are applied solidification and diffusion. And the CALPHAD, phase-field and kinetic Monte Carlo methods of Part X are computational versions of the thermodynamics and kinetics introduced here. The laws of thermodynamics themselves are taken as known from the Advanced Chemistry book; Part II applies them to solids, solutions, interfaces and defects.
