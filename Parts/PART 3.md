# Part III — Mechanical Behaviour

Every engineered object carries load: a bridge girder carries traffic, a turbine disc the pull of its blades at high temperature, a hip implant several times body weight a million times a year. Part II explained how thermodynamics and kinetics set a material's structure. Part III asks what that structure does when a force is applied: how much the material stretches, when it stops springing back, how far it can deform before it breaks, and how its behaviour changes with time, temperature and repeated loading.

The answers run through a single chain of ideas. Elastic stiffness comes from the curvature of the interatomic bond and is almost insensitive to microstructure. Plastic flow in crystals comes from the motion of dislocations, and is therefore extremely sensitive to microstructure: every grain boundary, solute atom, precipitate and other dislocation in the way raises the strength. Fracture comes from cracks, and is governed by the competition between the energy released when a crack grows and the energy the material can absorb at the crack tip. Creep, viscoelasticity and fatigue come from thermally activated processes and damage accumulated cycle by cycle. With that chain in hand, a steel bolt, a polymer housing, a zirconia hip head and a jet-engine disc become applications of the same physics.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- How stress and strain are described as tensors, how elastic constants are linked to bonding and crystal symmetry, and what the standard mechanical tests actually measure.
- Why real crystals yield at a thousandth of their theoretical strength, and how the stress fields, forces, interactions and multiplication of dislocations explain it.
- How slip on specific crystal planes produces macroscopic yielding, and how work hardening, solutes, grain boundaries and precipitates raise strength — and how these mechanisms combine in commercial alloys.
- How materials deform slowly under sustained load (creep) and how polymers combine elastic and viscous response.
- How cracks control fracture through $K$, $G$ and $J$, why some materials change from ductile to brittle on cooling, how fatigue cracks start and grow, and why brittle strength must be treated statistically.

Chapter 11 sets up the continuum language and the test methods that every later chapter uses. Chapter 12 descends to the defect that controls crystal plasticity, building on the introduction to dislocations in Chapter 5. Chapter 13 climbs back to the macroscopic scale, turning dislocation behaviour into yield criteria and strengthening rules that alloy designers use. Chapter 14 adds what the earlier chapters hold fixed — time and cracks — and so reaches the failure modes that end real components' lives.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part III depends on Parts I and II: crystal structures and symmetry (Chapters 2–4) fix slip systems and elastic anisotropy; defects (Chapter 5) introduce dislocations and grain boundaries; diffusion (Chapter 8) controls climb and creep; and the transformations of Chapter 10 create the precipitates, martensite and grain structures whose strengthening effects are explained here.
