# Part IX — Corrosion, Degradation and Surfaces

Most engineering materials do not fail because they were too weak on the day they entered service. They fail because they changed while in service. A buried pipeline thins at a fraction of a millimetre a year until it leaks; a stainless-steel fitting in a coastal plant pits and then cracks under a stress it carried easily when new; a polymer window frame chalks and embrittles in sunlight; a bridge deck spalls as chlorides reach its reinforcement; a gearbox loses its precision as its surfaces wear. Corrosion alone costs industrial economies a few percent of their gross domestic product every year, and much of that cost could be avoided with knowledge that already exists. This part treats the processes by which materials degrade through their surfaces and over time, together with the methods used to predict them, prevent them and work out why a component failed.

The part begins with the electrochemistry of corrosion, the most widespread degradation process, and its high-temperature counterpart, oxidation in hot gas. It then catalogues the forms corrosion takes and the ways engineers protect against them. It widens the view to degradation of every class of material and to the discipline of failure analysis. It ends at the surface itself, where adhesion, friction and wear are decided.

**By the end of this part you should understand:**

- why metals corrode, how electrode potentials and Pourbaix diagrams predict when corrosion is possible, and how mixed-potential theory and Tafel kinetics predict how fast it proceeds;
- how corrosion currents convert to rates of metal loss, and why passive films make stainless steels, aluminium and titanium usable;
- how oxide scales grow at high temperature, and what decides whether a scale protects or spalls;
- the forms of localized and environmentally assisted attack (galvanic, pitting, crevice, intergranular, stress-corrosion cracking, hydrogen embrittlement, corrosion fatigue) and the material, design, coating, inhibitor and cathodic-protection measures that counter them;
- how metals, ceramics, polymers, composites and concrete degrade, how lives are predicted from accelerated tests and reliability statistics, and how a failure investigation moves from evidence to root cause;
- how surface energy, adsorption and real contact area govern adhesion, friction, wear and lubrication, and how surfaces are engineered.

The fracture mechanics and fatigue of Chapter 14 reappear when a corrosive environment helps a crack grow, and the alloys of Chapters 15 and 16 are largely defined by their corrosion behaviour (the chromium in stainless steel, the clad layers on aerospace aluminium). The energy and extreme-environment materials of Chapters 40 and 41 depend on the protective scales and hydrogen resistance developed here. Looking forward, coatings and surface treatments are made by the processes of Chapters 47–49, corrosion products and fracture surfaces are examined with the methods of Chapters 52–54, and the cost and lifetime of a material in service feed its selection (Chapter 58) and its environmental footprint (Chapter 59). Electrochemistry fundamentals, the Nernst equation and electrode kinetics, are taught in the Advanced Chemistry book; this part applies them to materials.
